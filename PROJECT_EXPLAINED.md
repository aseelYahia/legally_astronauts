# PAWPRINT MARS: Project Explained

A beginner-friendly technical report on what the project actually contains, generated by inspecting the real files on
2026-11-14 (commit `002765d`, branch `feature/pawprint-mars`). All numbers below were read from the project's files or
computed by running the project's own functions. Nothing is invented. Where something is assumed, estimated, missing or
not verifiable, the report says so in **bold**.

> **How to read this report.** Part 1 covers the data, Part 2 the math, Part 3 how data flows, Part 4 where the code is,
> Part 5 how we checked things, Part 6 what works, and Part 7 practice questions for the judges.

## 0. Glossary (read this first)

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| **DTM / DEM** | Digital Terrain (Elevation) Model: a grid (raster) where every cell stores a ground height in metres. |
| **Raster** | An image-like grid of numbers. Each cell ("pixel") covers a fixed square of ground. |
| **GeoTIFF** | A TIFF image file that also stores *where on the planet* the pixels are (its map projection and corner coordinates). |
| **Resolution / post spacing** | Ground size of one pixel, e.g. "20 m" means each cell is 20 m × 20 m. |
| **CTX** | Context Camera on NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO). Medium resolution, about 6 m/pixel. |
| **HiRISE** | High Resolution Imaging Science Experiment on MRO. Very high resolution, about 25 cm/pixel imagery and 1 m DTMs. |
| **TRN** | Terrain Relative Navigation: the Mars 2020 landing system. USGS built the maps used here for TRN. |
| **Areoid** | Mars' "sea level", a reference surface of equal gravity potential. Elevations here are relative to it, so they are negative in Jezero (about −2,570 m). |
| **Planetocentric latitude** | Latitude measured from Mars' centre. Used by Mars 2020. |
| **East-positive longitude** | Longitude grows eastward from 0° to 360°. Jezero is about 77.4° E. |
| **eqc (Equidistant Cylindrical)** | A simple map projection: x = R·longitude, y = R·latitude (angles in radians). |
| **Sol** | One Mars day (about 24 h 39 min). Sol 0 is Perseverance's landing day. |
| **RMC** | Rover Motion Counter, e.g. `3_110` = site 3, drive 110. It identifies a rover position. |
| **Ls** | Solar longitude: Mars' position around the Sun, which tells you the season. |
| **LMST** | Local Mean Solar Time at the rover. |
| **AOI** | Area Of Interest: the small prototype region we process. |
| **Median / MAD** | Median = the middle value. MAD (median absolute deviation) = the median distance from the median, a "typical spread" that outliers barely affect. |
| **A\*** | ("A-star") A shortest-path search algorithm that uses a "guess of remaining cost" to search faster. |

### The big picture in one picture

```
 NASA / USGS servers                          Python (once)                     Python (every route)       Browser
 ───────────────────                          ─────────────                     ────────────────────       ───────

```

# PART 1: DATASETS

The prototype covers a small **Area Of Interest (AOI)**: latitude **18.42° N to 18.51° N** and longitude **77.34° E to
77.47° E** (defined in `analysis/pawprint/sources.py:11`). That is about **7.7 km × 5.3 km** around the Perseverance
landing site, Séítah and the western delta front. A smaller **HiRISE window** covers **18.42–18.465° N, 77.42–77.47° E**
(`sources.py:13`), about 3.0 km × 2.7 km around the landing site.

All raw files are in `pawprint-mars/data/raw/`:

## 2.0 Coordinates and ground spacing (used by everything)

**Input:** degrees (lon, lat) or projected metres (x, y). **File:** `analysis/pawprint/mars.py`.

| Formula | Plain English | Function |
|---|---|---|
| x = R · lon·π/180,  y = R · lat·π/180 | Convert degrees to projection metres | `lonlat_to_eqc` (line 20) |

20 × 0.94863 = **18.97 m** wide on the ground (the project stores 18.9726 m for that row). Ignoring this would make
east-west slopes about 5% too gentle.

**Why an astronaut cares:** a 5% error in distance becomes a 5% error in walking time and oxygen planning.

## 2.2 Terrain slope (Horn's method)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Input** | Elevation grid, the ground spacing per row (`dx_rows`), and `dy` (= 20 m for CTX) |
| **Where** | `analysis/pawprint/terrain.py:28` `slope_aspect()` |
| **Output** | A grid of slope angles in degrees (stored in `ctx_grid.npz` → `slope`) |

**Formula.** Label a 3×3 neighbourhood:

```
a b c        (north row)
d e f        e = the cell we compute slope for
g h i        (south row)
```

- dz/dx (east) = [(c + 2f + i) − (a + 2d + g)] / (8 · dx)
- dz/dy (south) = [(g + 2h + i) − (a + 2b + c)] / (8 · dy)
- slope = arctan( √( (dz/dx)² + (dz/dy)² ) ), converted to degrees

*In English:* compare the right column with the left column (east-west tilt) and the bottom row with the top row
(north-south tilt). The middle row/column counts double. Combine both tilts to get the steepest tilt.

**Real example: landing-site cell** (row 193, col 328, dx = 18.9726 m, dy = 20 m):

```
a −2565.673   b −2566.719   c −2567.898
d −2566.399   e −2567.192   f −2568.127
g −2566.920   h −2567.475   i −2568.184
```

- c+2f+i = −10272.336 ; a+2d+g = −10265.391 → difference −6.945 → ÷ (8 × 18.9726 = 151.78) → dz/dx = **−0.04576**
- g+2h+i = −10270.054 ; a+2b+c = −10267.009 → difference −3.045 → ÷ 160 → dz/dy = **−0.01904**
- gradient = √(0.04576² + 0.01904²) = **0.04956** → arctan → **2.84°** (the project reports 2.8371°)

**Other real cells:**

| Cell | Where | Slope |
|---|---|---|
| row 94, col 270 | delta front, 77.43124° E, 18.47809° N | **19.68°** |
| row 112, col 257 | steepest CTX cell, 77.42685° E, 18.47202° N | **33.23°** |

AOI statistics (CTX): mean slope 3.98°, 95th percentile 11.19°, max 33.23°.

**Why an astronaut cares:** slope is the main driver of fall risk, energy use and walking speed.

**Limitations:** slope depends heavily on resolution. On the 20 m grid, cliffs and small scarps are smoothed out, so
**real local slopes are steeper**. In the HiRISE window, the 2 m slope 95th percentile is 9.85°, and the 4 m routing grid
reaches up to 47.75° (it keeps the *maximum* 2 m slope in each 4 m cell). Edge cells reuse their own values ("edge
padding"), so slopes on the AOI border are slightly underestimated.

### 2.2b Aspect (computed, not used)
aspect = atan2(−dz/dx, dz/dy), in degrees clockwise from north, giving the direction the slope faces downhill. The landing
cell faces **112.6°** (east-south-east). **It is computed but not used anywhere** in the app or the router.

## 2.3 Terrain roughness (Terrain Ruggedness Index, Riley et al. 1999)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Input** | Elevation grid |
| **Formula** | TRI = √( (1/8) · Σ (zᵢ − z_centre)² ) over the 8 neighbours |
| **Where** | `terrain.py:50` `roughness_rms()` |
| **Output** | A grid in metres. **Only its AOI average (1.23 m) is shown** in Science Data. |

*In English:* how much do the 8 surrounding cells differ from the centre, on average (root-mean-square)?

**Real example (landing cell):** differences from −2567.192: +1.519, +0.473, −0.706, +0.793, −0.935, +0.272, −0.283, −0.992.
Squares: 2.307, 0.224, 0.498, 0.629, 0.874, 0.074, 0.080, 0.984 → sum 5.670 → ÷ 8 = 0.709 → √ = **0.842 m** (project: 0.8419 m).
At the steepest cell, TRI = 10.85 m.

**Limitations:** on a tilted but perfectly smooth plane TRI is still non-zero, so **TRI mixes slope with true
bumpiness**. **It is not used for routing.**

## 2.4 Hillshade (shaded relief)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Input** | Elevation, dx, dy; sun azimuth 315° (from the north-west), sun altitude 45° |

## 2.9 Walking speed and time (Tobler's hiking function), an **ESTIMATE**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Formula** | speed (km/h) = 6 · exp(−3.5 · \|g + 0.05\|), where g = Δz / horizontal (grade). Converted to m/s by ÷ 3.6. |
| **Step time** | t = 3-D length / speed × hazard multiplier (2.10) |
| **Where** | `routing.py:30` `tobler_speed_ms`; inlined in A* at `routing.py:142` |

*In English:* people walk fastest on a slight downhill (g = −0.05, 6 km/h = 1.667 m/s) and slow down exponentially on
steeper up or down slopes.

| grade | speed (m/s) |
|---:|---:|
| −0.05 | 1.667 (fastest) |
| 0.00 | 1.399 |
| +0.05 | 1.175 |
| +0.10 | 0.986 |
| +0.20 | 0.695 |
| −0.20 | 0.986 |

**Real step:** step 1 above: g = 1.518 / 27.567 = 0.0551 → v = 1.1538 m/s → t = 27.609 / 1.1538 × 1.0 = **23.93 s**.
**Whole route: 2,190.7 s ≈ 36.5 min** (the app shows "37 min").

> **Major limitations (must say this at the presentation):**
> 1. Tobler's function was fitted to **Earth hikers**, not suited astronauts in 0.38 g. **It is not validated for Mars EVA.**
> 2. The reported "walk time" **includes the hazard multiplier**, so it is a *penalised* time, not a pure walking time.
> 3. No rest stops, science stops, suit limits or consumables are modelled.

## 2.10 Hazard multiplier (route penalty)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Formula** | m = 1 if slope < 10°; otherwise m = 1 + ((slope − 10) / 10)² |
| **Where** | `routing.py:34` `hazard_multiplier`; precomputed per cell at `routing.py:98` |

| terrain slope | 0–10° | 12° | 15° | 20° | 25° | 30° |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| multiplier | 1.00 | 1.04 | 1.25 | 2.00 | 3.25 | 5.00 |

It uses the **Horn slope of the cell being entered**, which is the steepest tilt in any direction. That means it
penalises side-slopes as well as uphill. **Note:** the docstring at the top of `routing.py` calls this a "cross-terrain"
factor, but the code does not separate cross-slope from along-track slope. It uses the total slope.
> **ASSUMPTION:** the shape and constants of this formula are a design choice, not derived from data.

## 2.11 A* route planning

**Where:** `analysis/pawprint/routing.py:80` `astar()`, wrapped by `plan_route()` at line 207.

**Graph:**
- **Nodes** = grid cells (CTX 267 × 385 = 102,795 cells at 20 m; or HiRISE 666 × 741 = 493,506 cells at 4 m).
- **Edges** = moves to the 8 neighbours (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW). `_NEIGHBOURS` is at line 77.
- **Blocked cells** = NoData, or slope > max slope (default 25°, set by the app's slider between 10° and 35°; the API
  accepts 5–45°). **The start and goal cells are always allowed**, even if steep.
- **Edge cost g** = step time from 2.9 × multiplier from 2.10 (seconds).

**Heuristic h** ("optimistic guess of the remaining cost") = straight-line horizontal distance to the goal ÷ 1.667 m/s
(the fastest possible Tobler speed). Because no real step can be faster than 1.667 m/s, and multipliers are ≥ 1, h never
overestimates. This property is called **admissible**, and it guarantees A* returns the **cheapest** path for this cost model.

**Algorithm (in plain steps):**
1. Put the start in a priority queue (a heap) with priority f = g + h.
2. Repeatedly take the cell with the smallest f.
3. If it is the goal, follow the "came from" pointers back to the start, and you have the path.
4. Otherwise look at the 8 neighbours. If going through the current cell gives a neighbour a cheaper g, record it and push it.
5. If the queue empties, there is no route under the slope limit (the API returns HTTP 422).

**Real example (landing → Bearwallow, CTX):** start cell (193, 328), goal (152, 195). Heuristic at start = 2,653.3 m ÷ 1.667
= **1,591.9 s**. Actual optimal cost found = **2,190.7 s**. As expected, h ≤ actual.

**Multiple stops:** `plan_route` runs A* separately for each leg (start→waypoint→…→destination) and joins the legs. Each leg
is optimal, and so is the whole route, because legs are independent.

**Which grid?** `server.py:41` `choose_grid`: if **every** stop is inside the HiRISE window, it uses the 4 m HiRISE grid;
otherwise the 20 m CTX grid.

**Speed (measured):** HiRISE routes of 1.3–2.0 km took **0.18–0.96 s** after an optimisation (flat Python lists instead of
dicts; same results, verified by the Dijkstra test).

**Limitations:** the optimal path is optimal **for our assumed cost model**, not "safest" in any certified sense. A 20 m grid
cannot see boulders. Paths are staircased.

# PART 5: SCIENTIFIC VALIDATION

## 5.1 The 353 rover-position check (CTX)

**What was done** (`build_layers.py:303–336`):
1. Take every rover waypoint inside the AOI that has an `elev_geoid`. There are **353**: 271 end-of-drive, 68 mid-drive,
   **12 interpolated**, 1 "site increment" and 1 "first localization".
2. For each, find the CTX cell whose centre is nearest (`sample_grid`, `build_layers.py:144`: just rounding, no interpolation).
3. Compute d = CTX elevation − rover elevation.

**5 real examples:**

| sol | RMC | Note | rover elev (m) | CTX elev (m) | d (m) |
|---:|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 13 | 3_0 | Site increment, no motion. | −2569.910 | −2567.192 | +2.718 |
| 14 | 3_110 | First localization! | −2569.862 | −2567.192 | +2.670 |
| 15 | 3_386 | End-of-drive localization | −2569.935 | −2567.898 | +2.037 |
| 360 | 10_516 | Mid-drive localization | −2569.205 | −2567.919 | +1.286 |
| 360 | 10_1114 | End-of-drive localization | −2569.222 | −2569.063 | +0.159 |

**How the statistics are calculated:**
- **Median** = sort all 353 values of d and take the middle one → **−0.15 m** (no overall bias).
- **MAD** = take |d − median| for every point, then the median of those → **1.57 m** (typical disagreement).
- **P5 / P95** = the values below which 5% / 95% of the d values fall → **−3.76 m / +4.79 m**.
- For comparison: mean −0.01 m, standard deviation 2.51 m, min −6.88 m, max +6.58 m; 58% of points agree within 2 m.
- Excluding the 12 interpolated points changes the median to −0.07 m (n = 341), a negligible difference.

## 5.2 What this check really means (important)

The **HiRISE vs rover** check gives a median of **0.001 m** and a MAD of **0.011 m** over 119 points. Agreement this tight
(about 1 cm) cannot happen between two independent measurements. It tells us **NASA's rover elevations are tied to this
same HiRISE TRN DTM**. Therefore:

- ✔ It **proves** our HiRISE window is cut, positioned and referenced correctly (no pixel shift, correct datum).
- ✔ The CTX check effectively measures **CTX vs HiRISE along the traverse** (median −0.15 m, MAD 1.57 m).
- ✘ It does **not** independently measure the absolute accuracy of the DTMs.
- Separately, HiRISE vs CTX over the whole window (19,832 CTX cells): median **−1.37 m**, MAD 1.59 m. USGS reports a
  −0.4 m median offset over its full mosaic. **The 1 m gap in our small window is unexplained.**

## 5.3 How the slope thresholds were chosen
They were **not derived from data or from a NASA standard**. They are round planning numbers: 10° (start of "moderate"),
15° (matching the mock-up's "Slope hazards > 15°"), and 25° (default no-go). The app labels them **ASSUMPTION**. Before
claiming they are realistic for suited astronauts, they should be compared with published EVA or Apollo traverse literature.
**That comparison has not been done.**

## 5.4 Scientific support

| Calculation | Support |
|---|---|
| eqc ↔ degrees, Mars radius | Exact, from the GeoTIFF's own CRS; unit-tested |
| Haversine distance | Standard spherical geometry; unit-tested |
| cos(lat) ground correction | Exact property of the eqc projection; unit-tested |
| Horn slope | Standard GIS method (Horn 1981); tested on planes with known answers |
| TRI roughness | Published index (Riley et al. 1999); tested |
| Hillshade | Standard illumination model; tested |
| A\* optimality | Proven property with an admissible heuristic; tested against Dijkstra |
| Georeferencing / datum | Verified against rover localizations (cm-level) |

## 5.5 Project assumptions
Slope classes; the 25° default limit; the hazard multiplier formula; using Tobler (Earth) for Mars; the difficulty score
weights; the risk rule; the 400 m "nearby" radius; sample positions = last fix before sealing; elevation colours.

## 5.6 Cannot currently be verified
Absolute DTM accuracy (no independent ground truth used); real astronaut walking speeds on Mars; whether 25° is a safe
limit; exact sample coordinates; hazards smaller than the grid (rocks, sand traps, soft soil); surface material (no CRISM,
no thermal inertia); current weather.

## 5.7 What the tests prove and do not prove
- **They prove:** the formulas give the right numbers on known geometry; A* is optimal for its cost model; the API
  behaves correctly; the processed data is consistent with NASA's rover positions; and bounds, units and labels are right.
- **They do not prove:** that the routes are *safe*, that the time estimates are *realistic*, that the thresholds are
  *appropriate*, or that the DTM is accurate beyond the metre level. There are no UI/browser tests (the UI was checked
  manually in a browser).

# PART 7: LIKELY JUDGES' QUESTIONS

1. **Is the terrain real?** Yes. It is the USGS Mars 2020 TRN CTX DTM (20 m, DOI 10.5066/P906QQT8) and a 1 m window of the
   TRN HiRISE DTM (DOI 10.5066/P9REJ9JN), built from MRO stereo images for Perseverance's landing.

2. **What coordinate system do you use?** The products' own Equidistant Cylindrical projection on a 3,396,190 m Mars sphere,
   with planetocentric latitude, east-positive longitude and elevations above the areoid. The browser map uses those
   degrees directly, which is exact for this projection.

3. **How do you calculate slope?** Horn's 3×3 method, with the east-west pixel size corrected by cos(latitude) (about 5% at
   Jezero). It is tested on synthetic planes with known slopes.

4. **How accurate is your elevation?** Our grids agree with NASA's rover-localization elevations to a median of −0.15 m
   (MAD 1.6 m) for CTX and about 1 cm for HiRISE. The 1 cm match shows NASA's localizations use the same HiRISE DTM, so
   this confirms our georeferencing, **not** independent absolute accuracy.

5. **Why A\*?** It finds the cheapest path on a grid much faster than exhaustive search. Our heuristic (straight-line
   distance ÷ maximum walking speed) never overestimates, so the result is optimal for our cost model. A test compares it
   with Dijkstra.

6. **What does the cost represent?** Estimated time: Tobler's hiking speed for each step's grade, multiplied by a penalty
   that grows with terrain slope above 10°. Cells steeper than the limit (default 25°) are blocked.

7. **Is Tobler valid on Mars?** No. It was fitted to Earth hikers. We use it to rank routes, and label the time as an estimate.

8. **Where do 10/15/25° come from?** They are our planning assumptions, labelled as such in the app. They are not NASA
   requirements, and validating them against EVA literature is future work.

9. **Can your planner see boulders?** No. The 20 m grid cannot. The 4 m HiRISE grid near the landing site sees much more
   (we keep the steepest 2 m slope inside each 4 m cell to stay cautious), but rocks under about 2 m are still invisible.

10. **Where do the sample locations come from?** Names, sols and types come from NASA's sample page. NASA doesn't publish
    coordinates there, so we place each sample at the rover's last localization on or before the sealing sol (2–23 sols
    earlier). They are marked "estimated position".

11. **Is the weather live?** No. NASA's public MEDA feed stopped at sol 1133 (April 2024). We show it as archived, and
    weather is not used in route calculations.

12. **How did you handle the 1.8 GB HiRISE file?** We parsed its TIFF strip table and downloaded only our window's bytes
    with HTTP range requests (31.6 MB), then cross-checked pixels against GDAL.

13. **Why are some routes longer than the straight line?** Grid moves are limited to 8 directions, and the planner detours
    around steep cells. Example: landing → Bearwallow is 2.88 km by route vs 2.66 km straight.

14. **What's real and what's estimated in the metrics panel?** Distance, elevation gain/loss and slopes are DERIVED from
    real DTMs. Walk time and the difficulty score are ESTIMATES. Each value carries a badge.

15. **What would you add next?** Independent accuracy checks, CRISM mineral layers, a larger HiRISE coverage area, a
    rock-abundance or roughness cost, a validated EVA metabolic model instead of Tobler, smoothed paths, and route export (GeoJSON/KML).
